A Modality-Aware Spatiotemporal Fusion Framework for Brent Crude Oil Forecasting Using Maritime Networks, Satellite Imagery and Financial Time Series

Chapter 6 Conclusion

Short-term oil price volatility affects budgeting, hedging and market-risk decisions, yet weekly Brent forecasts remain difficult to improve on a simple no-change rule. Using weekly data from 2019 to 2025, this study examined whether satellite remote sensing and maritime shipping provide predictive information beyond conventional financial indicators. It also tested whether modality-aware representation-level modelling performs better than flat feature fusion. Under a common evaluation framework, each added data source was compared with both a finance-only model and the no-change benchmark.

The results show that adding more data or using more complex models does not guarantee better forecasting performance. Remote sensing provides no consistent improvement, either when added alone or together with shipping data. Shipping provides clearer incremental predictive value, although this improvement is mainly observed in the Deep pathway. In the main run, the Deep model using financial and shipping data records a modest improvement over the no-change benchmark, while none of the Flat models outperforms the benchmark. Across the same information sets, the Deep pathway generally performs better than the Flat models. This supports the value of preserving the structure of different data types and modelling them at the representation level. Model interpretation further shows that forecasts continue to rely mainly on financial and EIA information. Shipping makes a smaller contribution, and its importance varies across disruption types, periods and locations in the transport network.

The main contribution of this study is to provide a framework and set of criteria for assessing when new types of data provide meaningful predictive value. By comparing each new data source with both established financial information and a simple no-change benchmark, the framework distinguishes incremental value from overall forecasting performance. The study also shows that the value of a data source should be assessed according to how well its scale, frequency and structure match the analytical task. Remote sensing may be more suitable for monitoring facility activity or regional production, while shipping data are better suited to capturing disruptions and adjustments across global supply networks. Monitoring value and predictive value should therefore be assessed separately. For applications such as energy-security monitoring, trade planning and inflation-sensitive budgeting, spatial data such as remote sensing and shipping can complement conventional market information by revealing facility activity, transport disruptions, rerouting and changes in supply networks. The value of spatial data therefore extends beyond price forecasting to the monitoring of physical activity and the identification of potential risks. However, these signals should be assessed together with other market information.

Future research can extend this study in three areas: temporal and spatial coverage, forecasting tasks, and practical applications. First, a longer time span and broader geographic coverage would help test whether the value of shipping information remains stable across different periods and regions. Second, the same research framework could be applied to other energy and commodity markets, including WTI crude oil, LNG, natural gas, grains and metals. Remote sensing could also be evaluated for tasks that better match its spatial and temporal scale. Finally, future research should further assess the practical value of the framework, including its use in energy-security monitoring, trade planning and budgeting. Evaluating specific policies would require combining the forecasting framework with causal or decision-analysis methods.
